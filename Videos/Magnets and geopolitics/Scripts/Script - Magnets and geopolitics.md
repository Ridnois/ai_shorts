## 2. Script

*Voice notes: measured, warm, without dramatising. A one-second pause between paragraphs and three between sections. The `[image]` are cues; the narration must be understood without seeing them.*

### Opening · the Chicago factory (≈ 1 min)

At the end of May 2025, in a plant in Chicago, an assembly line stopped.

Steel was not missing. There was no strike, nor a fire. A very small part was missing, one that a supplier could no longer deliver: a magnet.

[pause]

The plant made the Ford Explorer, and companies in the sector said the stoppage lasted about a week. A car like that uses magnets in places almost nobody thinks of: the brakes, the steering, the electric seats, the fuel injectors.

[image: a simple illustration of an open car, with small dots marking where there are magnets]

This story is about that magnet. About what it is, why it is so hard to replace and why, for some years now, it is discussed in meetings between presidents.

We will go in parts. First, what is special about it. Then, how a single country came to produce almost all of them. Then, what happened in 2025. And at the end, what other countries are trying to do, and why it takes so long.

### Part 1 · What this magnet is (≈ 3 min)

Let us start with the simple thing.

A magnet is a material that creates a magnetic field without needing electricity. For centuries, magnets were weak. Pieces of iron, or of common alloys, that served for a compass or to stick a note on the refrigerator.

[image: a very simple timeline, with a compass on the left]

That changed in 1984. That year, two research groups, one at General Motors, in the United States, and another at the Japanese company Sumitomo, discovered almost at the same time a new alloy of neodymium, iron and boron. They worked separately, and both arrived at the same compound.

This alloy produces much stronger magnets than the earlier ones. With the same weight, a neodymium magnet does a job that used to demand a piece several times bigger.

And that difference matters for a concrete reason. An electric motor works thanks to magnets. If the magnets are more powerful, the motor can be smaller, lighter and more efficient.

[pause]

That is why these magnets ended up in many different objects. The motors of electric and hybrid cars. Cordless tools. Hard drives. Speakers and headphones. And the generators of wind turbines.

An example for a sense of scale: the electric motors of a Toyota Prius are estimated to contain about a kilo of neodymium. One kilo, in a car of over a thousand kilos. Little material, but without it, the motor does not work the same.

There is one more detail, and it is the one that will matter later.

These magnets have a weak point: heat. Above about a hundred degrees, they lose strength. And a car motor or a turbine work, precisely, at high temperatures.

The solution is to add small amounts of two other elements: dysprosium and terbium. With them, the magnet withstands heat. They are scarce elements, and harder to obtain than neodymium itself.

[image: two coloured bars, one that drops with temperature and another that holds]

So we have a material that is small, expensive to replace and present in almost everything that moves with electricity. Let us now see where it comes from.

### Part 2 · How production concentrated (≈ 4 min)

These elements belong to a group called rare earths. The name confuses. They are not so rare in the earth's crust. The hard part is not finding them, but separating them.

Rare earths appear mixed, in minerals containing fifteen or more elements with almost identical chemical properties. Separating them demands many processing stages, with chemicals that pollute, and with an experience that takes decades to accumulate.

[image: a diagram of a mineral splitting into many coloured layers]

During a good part of the twentieth century, the world's main producer was not China. It was a mine in the California desert, called Mountain Pass, discovered in 1949.

[image: a map with a dot in California]

But between 1985 and 1995, China raised its share of rare-earth production from around twenty percent to nearly sixty. It had large deposits, among them Bayan Obo, in Inner Mongolia, which holds most of its reserves. And it produced at a lower cost, partly because it asked less of companies on environmental matters.

Mountain Pass did not hold out. The company that operated it, Molycorp, declared bankruptcy in 2015, and the mine was put on pause. Two years later a group of investors bought it, among whom there was a Chinese company. [pause] It is a fact often mentioned, and it should be taken with care: we are talking about a minority stake within a consortium.

What matters is what happened in the meantime.

China had not only won in mining. It had won in what comes after: refining, and making the magnet.

The International Energy Agency published the figures for 2024. China concentrates around sixty percent of the extraction of the rare earths used in magnets. But it concentrates ninety-one percent of refining. And ninety-four percent of the production of sintered neodymium magnets.

[image: three horizontal bars, of 60, 91 and 94 percent, with the same fill colour]

Notice how the figure grows as we move toward the final product. In 2005, China produced about half of these magnets. Twenty years later, almost all.

This explains something that may surprise. Even if other countries had mines, the problem remained the same. The ore could be extracted outside China, but it had to be sent there to be refined, and then to make the magnet.

[pause]

Control of mining is visible, because a mine can be seen. Control of processing is silent, and it is the one that weighs most.

### Part 3 · When the magnet becomes an instrument (≈ 4 min)

A country producing almost all of a material is not, by itself, a conflict. It becomes one when that material is used as a tool of pressure.

The first episode mentioned happened in 2010. During a maritime dispute between China and Japan, it was reported that rare-earth shipments to Japan had been interrupted. China denied there was a formal ban. What is certain is that, at that moment, prices rose and Japan began looking for alternative suppliers.

[image: a map of the East China Sea, without marking borders precisely]

That was the first warning. Almost fifteen years later came the second, and it was broader.

On 4 April 2025, China put seven medium and heavy rare earths under export license. Among them, dysprosium and terbium, the ones that make the magnet withstand heat. And the magnets containing those elements came to need a permit from the Chinese government to leave the country.

An important nuance: neodymium itself was not on that list. The list touched dysprosium and terbium, and in that way reached the magnets that carry them.

The measure arrived while China and the United States were arguing over tariffs. Shipments fell quickly, and factories noticed before governments did.

There is the Chicago plant, which we spoke of at the beginning. A few weeks after the restrictions, a supplier ran out of magnets and the Explorer line stopped. On 26 May, Suzuki halted production of its Swift model in Japan, the first Japanese automaker directly affected. Ford's chief executive described the situation, in June, as one of "day-to-day" supply.

[image: a calendar with three marked dates: 4 April, mid-May, 26 May]

[pause]

None of these factories had done anything wrong. They depended on a cheap part, bought from a supplier, which depended in turn on another supplier.

In October 2025 came the third stage. On 9 October, China widened its controls. For the first time, the rule also reached products made outside China that contained material of Chinese origin, as well as processing technologies.

The International Energy Agency estimated the size of what was at stake: if those rules were applied in full, they put at risk about 6.5 trillion dollars of production a year outside China. Almost half, 3 trillion, was in the automotive sector.

[image: a big circle divided; the largest part, marked "cars"]

It is a figure from an estimate, not a measured fact. But it shows why something so small matters so much: magnets are not the product, they are what makes many other things possible.

Toward the end of October, after a meeting between the presidents of the United States and China in South Korea, China agreed to suspend that widening for a year. The April restrictions, however, remained in force.

### Part 4 · The response (≈ 4 min)

What can be done when such a dependence is discovered?

The answer seems simple: produce magnets in other places. In practice, it is the slowest thing of all.

We saw it with a US company, MP Materials, the one that operates Mountain Pass. In July 2025, the US Department of Defense agreed to invest 400 million dollars in shares of the company, and became its largest shareholder. In exchange, the company committed to building a magnet plant.

[image: two simple buildings, one saying "2025 · first plant" and another "2028 · second plant"]

According to an analysis published in early October 2026, the first plant started producing in December 2025, with the goal of reaching 3,000 tonnes a year by 2027. A second plant is expected for 2028. Together, they would reach about 10,000 tonnes a year.

For comparison: China's largest maker, JL MAG, aims at 60,000 tonnes in 2027.

[pause]

There is a finer difficulty, and it is that of the heavy elements. Lynas, the main producer outside China, reported for the first quarter of 2026 about eight tonnes of dysprosium and terbium. It is a start. But the demand for military and civil use is measured in thousands of tonnes.

An analyst summed it up with a phrase worth repeating: capital cannot buy forty years of process experience. A plant can be built in three years. What costs more is knowing how to make it run well, and that accumulates with practice.

And the tension continues. In June 2026, China added ten US companies, among them MP Materials, to its export-control lists, in response to new US sanctions. The conflict did not end with the suspension: it changed shape.

### Close · a date and a question (≈ 2 min)

When this episode was recorded, on 3 October 2026, there were two dates ahead.

The first: 10 November 2026, when the suspension of China's widened rules expires. As far as I could verify, there is no confirmed extension. It may be extended, it may apply only to some elements or it may return in full. We do not know.

The second: 1 January 2027, when a US Department of Defense rule enters into force prohibiting the use of Chinese-origin magnets in military purchases.

[image: a horizontal line with two marks separated by about seven weeks]

[pause]

Let us go back to the Chicago factory. What stopped there was not a big machine or a rare material. It was a small part, inside a system nobody had looked at closely.

Perhaps that is what this story shows. That a country's strength, today, is not always measured in what it extracts from the earth. Sometimes it is measured in what it knows how to do with it: in that long chain of steps that goes from ore to magnet, and that takes decades to learn.

And an open question remains. When such a common material becomes strategic, what other small objects, invisible today, occupy the same place?

[end · music at low volume, 10 seconds]

## 3. Visual language proposal (for you to decide)

Following your idea of coherence between layers (thumbnail, on-screen text, illustration):

- **Style:** flat, minimalist illustration, simple shapes, no real faces. Everything that looks like a real event (factories, people, presidents) is represented with **icons or maps**, never with realistic generated images.
- **Palette:** three or four fixed colours for the whole channel (for example, night blue as background, copper to highlight a datum, ivory for text).
- **On-screen text:** one figure or one word per scene, in the same typeface; never long sentences.
- **Thumbnail:** a single large object (here, a magnet), a plain background and three words at most.
- **Movement:** very slow (soft drifts), no quick cuts, so it accompanies the voice and can be listened to without looking.
- **Series:** the pattern "How did a small object…?" can be the brand's recurring format, like the example you gave of a series' style.

All this is a proposal: if you prefer another style (the "lego style" example, for instance), it only has to be fixed before the first episode and not changed.
